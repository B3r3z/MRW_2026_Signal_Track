\begin{abstract}
This paper presents SignalTrack, a modular experimental platform for distributed and mobile wireless systems based on software-defined radio (SDR). The platform combines multiple SDR-equipped mobile units with a centralized controller responsible for coordination, synchronization, and experiment orchestration \cite{MQTTv5}. A message-based publish–subscribe communication model enables dynamic configuration, scalable integration of nodes, and algorithm-agnostic operation. The proposed architecture is designed to support experiments in distributed beamforming, adaptive transmission, and integrated sensing and communication scenarios under realistic conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid development of advanced wireless communication and sensing systems has increased interest in experimental platforms based on software-defined radio (SDR). By implementing radio functionality primarily in software, SDR enables flexible prototyping and evaluation of adaptive transmission, beamforming, and integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) techniques under realistic conditions~\cite{Mitola1993SDR,Ulversoy2010SDR}. 


Many emerging applications, including distributed MIMO systems, cooperative mobile nodes, and environment-aware wireless sensing~\cite{Hassanien2016DFRC,Sahin2025PARC}, require experimental setups that support dynamic reconfiguration, spatial adaptability, and coordinated operation of multiple radio units~\cite{Goldsmith2003MIMO,Heath2016mmWave,Liu2020ISAC}. In such scenarios, the spatial relationship between transmitters and receivers, as well as their mobility, plays a critical role in overall system performance~\cite{AlHourani2014LAP}. However, conventional SDR-based testbeds are typically designed for static deployments with predefined node roles and limited runtime reconfigurability, which constrains their usefulness for evaluating distributed and mobile wireless systems~\cite{Ulversoy2010SDR,Tan2015Sora}.

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in platforms that combine centralized coordination with distributed radio nodes, allowing global optimization while preserving the local autonomy of individual units~\cite{Hassanien2016DFRC,Liu2020ISAC}. Centralized controllers can aggregate measurement data, execute computationally intensive algorithms, and coordinate transmission strategies across multiple nodes, while distributed radio units focus on real-time signal transmission, reception, and low-level control.

To address these requirements, this work presents SignalTrack, a modular and centrally coordinated experimental platform for distributed and mobile wireless systems. The platform integrates multiple SDR-equipped mobile units with a centralized controller responsible for synchronization, coordination, and experiment orchestration. Communication between system components is realized using a message-based publish–subscribe model, enabling flexible runtime reconfiguration and scalable integration of heterogeneous nodes.

The proposed architecture decouples radio operation, control logic, and experiment execution, allowing algorithm-agnostic operation and seamless switching between different experimental scenarios. As a result, the same platform can be used to investigate distributed beamforming, adaptive transmission strategies, cooperative sensing, and mobility-aware wireless experiments without structural modifications of the system.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:unit_architecture} describes the hardware architecture of the mobile unit. Section~\ref{sec:system_architecture} presents the system-level communication and control framework. Section~\ref{sec:integration} discusses the integration of the central system with the mobile units, and conclusions are drawn in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

Although each unit operates autonomously at the execution level, high-level coordination and decision-making are performed by the centralized system controller. The hardware architecture follows a layered design principle in which each subsystem operates at a level of abstraction appropriate to its real-time requirements and computational complexity. \subsection{Mobility Layer}
The drivetrain consists of two DC motors arranged in a differential-drive configuration, each connected to an H-bridge motor driver module. The H-bridge provides bidirectional control of motor current and allows pulse-width modulation (PWM) based speed regulation. Both H-bridge channels are driven by an ESP32 microcontroller, which serves as the low-level mobility controller.

The ESP32 executes a real-time control loop that processes PWM commands, reads the wheel encoder feedback, and acquires data from an onboard inertial measurement unit (IMU). The ESP32 is not involved in system-level decision-making and executes motion commands
received from the supervisory computing layer without local interpretation. These sensor inputs enable closed-loop velocity control and odometry estimation at the firmware level. This closed-loop operation is limited to low-level mobility control and operates independently of system-level radio processing and coordination logic. Additionally, the ESP32 implements safety mechanisms, including a software watchdog timer and emergency stop handling, ensuring that the mobility subsystem operates reliably and independently of higher-level software failures.

\subsection{Computation Layer}
The onboard computing platform is a single-board computer (SBC) with GPU acceleration, which serves as the supervisory controller for the entire mobile unit. The onboard computer communicates with the ESP32 via a wired serial interface (UART), through which it issues high-level motion commands (e.g., velocity setpoints, trajectory waypoints, turn commands) and receives telemetry data such as encoder counts, IMU readings, and battery status. The onboard computer acts as the interface between system-level coordination and low-level hardware execution, translating received commands into motion- or radio-specific actions.


The separation between the ESP32 and the onboard computer reflects a deliberate decomposition of real-time motor control (handled by the microcontroller) and computationally intensive tasks such as SDR signal processing (handled by the onboard computer). This two-tier approach ensures deterministic motor control while enabling flexible, software-defined behavior at the application level.

\subsection{Radio Layer}
Each mobile unit carries an SDR front-end connected to the onboard computer via USB~3.0. The onboard computer controls the SDR through a hardware driver framework, which provides programmatic access to transmission parameters, including carrier frequency, sampling rate, gain, and bandwidth. An omnidirectional antenna is mounted on the chassis to enable signal transmission.

The choice of the onboard computing platform is directly influenced by the signal processing demands imposed by the SDR front-end. The instantaneous bandwidth supported by the SDR determines the baseband sampling rate and, consequently, the volume of IQ samples that must be generated or processed per unit of time. For example, operating at a bandwidth of several MHz results in millions of complex samples per second, each requiring real-time waveform synthesis, buffering, and USB~3.0 streaming. These requirements necessitate a computing platform capable of sustained, low-latency numerical processing, which motivates the use of a GPU-accelerated single-board computer within the computation layer. The onboard computer hosts the transmit signal processing pipeline, including waveform generation, sample buffering, and SDR control via the hardware driver framework.

\section{System Architecture}

\label{sec:system_architecture}

The SignalTrack platform is organized as a centralized, message-driven experimental system that coordinates multiple mobile transmitter nodes and stationary receiver nodes under the supervision of a central controller. In this architecture, message exchange constitutes the primary interface between the system-level controller and all participating nodes.

The architecture explicitly separates three functional responsibilities: local hardware control at the node level, centralized coordination and decision-making, and algorithm-specific signal processing logic. This separation is enforced through message-based interfaces, allowing transmission strategies and control logic to be modified without changes to the underlying mobility or communication subsystems.


\subsection{Centralized Controller}

The centralized controller operates as the system master and is responsible for global synchronization, experiment orchestration, and closed-loop control. It runs on a dedicated central PC connected to the same local network as all TX and RX nodes, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_architecture}. The controller maintains a global view of the system state and coordinates the interaction between distributed nodes at the control and experimental management level. All coordination and control actions are mediated by the centralized controller, and no direct peer-to-peer communication between mobile or receiver nodes is required. 

System startup follows a structured two-stage initialization procedure. First, each TX and RX node registers with the controller, allowing it to verify the expected system topology. This registration phase establishes the system topology exclusively through message exchange, without assumptions about static node configuration. After registration, nodes independently initialize their local hardware resources, including SDR front-ends and onboard processing pipelines, and report readiness to the controller. Only after all expected nodes have entered the ready state does the controller initiate the experiment by broadcasting a global start command. The global start command acts as a synchronization barrier, ensuring that all nodes
begin transmission or reception only after system-wide readiness is confirmed. This mechanism ensures that no transmission or reception begins before the system is fully synchronized. 

Once the experiment is running, the controller receives measurement data from the RX nodes, executes centralized processing and control logic, and issues updated transmission parameters to the TX nodes. This design enables coordinated transmission strategies while avoiding direct peer-to-peer dependencies between nodes.

\subsection{Stationary Receiver Nodes}

In addition to the mobile transmitter units, the system includes two stationary receiver nodes. Each receiver consists of a computer platform equipped with an SDR front-end operating in receive mode. The receiver nodes capture baseband IQ samples of the over-the-air signals transmitted by the mobile units and forward these samples to the central controller for centralized processing.

The fixed and known spatial placement of the receivers provides stable reference positions, facilitating calibration procedures and enabling reproducible spatial analysis. The use of two spatially separated receivers enables spatial diversity and supports the analysis of received signal characteristics across different observation points.


\subsection{Distributed Transmission Control Architecture}

In the initial operational configuration, the system is designed to support distributed transmission scenarios involving multiple mobile transmitter units. Each SDR front-end is mounted on a separate mobile unit and operates as an independent transmitter under centralized coordination.

The centralized controller collects measurement data from the stationary receivers and can apply centralized processing to derive transmission parameter updates for individual transmitters. 
This architecture enables experimental investigation of phase-coherent transmission strategies, such as distributed beamforming. While transmission is physically distributed across mobile units, all coordination
and parameter adaptation are performed centrally.

The current implementation focuses on establishing the communication,
synchronization, and control infrastructure, while advanced transmission strategies,
such as distributed beamforming, are considered as part of ongoing and future work.



\subsection{Scalability and Extensibility}
The modular design of both the mobile units and the communication infrastructure allows the system to scale beyond four transmitters or two receivers without architectural changes. Additional nodes can join the network by registering with the centralized controller. The MQTT-based communication model enables dynamic addition and removal of nodes, supporting experiments with variable system scale and heterogeneous configurations.



\section{Integration of the Central System with Mobile Units}
\label{sec:integration}




The integration of the central control system with the mobile units is realized through the reception and interpretation of control messages. No direct coupling between the system logic and the mobile hardware is assumed beyond
this message-based interface. The hardware-level organization of this integration, including the network
infrastructure and the internal structure of the mobile unit, is illustrated
in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_architecture}. The sequence of message exchanges between the system components is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:UML}.
The onboard computer acts as a gateway, directing specific commands to the appropriate subsystems based on the message content. This architecture ensures that instructions related to physical movement are routed to the embedded motion controller, while parameters concerning signal generation are directed to the software-defined radio frontend.

Within each mobile unit, message-based communication constitutes the primary interface between system-level control and local hardware execution. The low-level mobility execution is handled by an embedded microcontroller (e.g., ESP32), which operates independently of the radio subsystem. This layer receives interpreted motion commands from the main message stream, ensuring that navigation tasks are executed reliably without burdening the high-level signal processing unit. The microcontroller executes only validated motion commands and does not participate in algorithmic decision-making or system-level coordination.

The onboard computer serves as the local execution point for system-level commands within each mobile unit. It hosts the software modules that interface with the SDR hardware via the corresponding driver framework, translating high-level system commands into specific waveform configurations and transmission timings.

Although motion control and radio operations are handled by distinct hardware components, the message handling architecture unifies them into a coherent system. This design allows both subsystems to be synchronized using a unified control flow, while preserving their independent execution paths. The communication framework ensures that a single control stream can efficiently synchronize vehicle positioning with radio transmission, maintaining a logical separation of duties while preserving system-wide consistency.

Connectivity with the controller of the central system is achieved through a publish–subscribe model based on the MQTT protocol. This registration process establishes a communication contract between the mobile unit and the central controller, defining its role and available capabilities. Upon startup, each mobile unit registers its capabilities and reports readiness only after the successful initialization of both the SDR hardware and internal processing pipelines. During operation, the central controller issues experimental parameters, while the nodes return status updates or collected metrics.



The message-based architecture supports various experimental scenarios, which may require different data exchange schemes. Depending on the specific algorithm loaded on the central controller, the message flow can be adapted ranging from simple beacon transmission to complex, feedback-driven configurations without requiring changes to the underlying mobile unit firmware.




In summary, the architecture relies on a versatile message exchange protocol that enables a flexible binding between the central system logic and the distributed mobile hardware. This approach facilitates scalable coordination by abstracting the complexity of simultaneous motion and radio control into a unified communication interface. 



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper presents SignalTrack, a modular experimental platform designed for the study of distributed and mobile wireless systems under centralized coordination. The platform integrates mobile SDR-enabled transmitter units, stationary receiver nodes, and a central controller responsible for synchronization, data aggregation, and algorithm execution. By decoupling radio operation, mobility control, and experiment logic, the proposed architecture enables flexible reconfiguration and supports a wide range of experimental scenarios without requiring structural modifications.

The presented architecture emphasizes scalable coordination of distributed mobile SDR units through message-based system integration. The separation between real-time hardware control and high-level signal processing simplifies system integration and facilitates reproducible experimentation in distributed transmission and sensing scenarios. The presented design focuses on architectural principles and system organization rather than on specific signal processing outcomes.

Future work will focus on the systematic evaluation of the platform under different experimental configurations. This includes the integration and assessment of various control and signal processing algorithms, exploration of coordinated multi-transmitter transmission strategies, and investigation of sensing-driven adaptation mechanisms. Additional efforts will consider extending the system to larger node counts, incorporating mobility-aware coordination, and analyzing timing, synchronization, and scalability aspects in more complex deployments.
